Today, through my eyes

Baby fish hide between my roots where nothing bigger can reach them. A heron stands so still on my branch I forget it's there. The tide comes in like it's greeting an old friend. I have stood here for years. I will stand here for many more.

One day the water reaching me is full of chemicals, from a spill somewhere upstream. It burns the roots that breathe for me, and they stop working, one after another. I'm rooted to this spot, I can't move and I have no voice. I can't warn the fish hiding in the mud beside me, or the birds who eat them, or the people further downstream, who won't know there was a spill until the fish start dying too.

Did you know?

Victoria has only one native mangrove species. Its pencil roots rise from the mud like snorkels, helping the buried roots breathe.

Melbourne’s “white mangrove” is the tree also called the grey mangrove. Around Port Phillip and Western Port, its roots connect sheltered tidal mud, coastal water and a rich food web.

One Victorian mangrove, two names

Both names describe the same species, Avicennia marina. The accepted name for the Victorian subspecies is Avicennia marina subsp. australasica; the older “var. australasica” still appears in some reports and field guides. Its bark is grey, and the leaf undersides are covered with fine grey-white hairs. Salt secreted through the leaves can add a crystalline coating. The tree belongs to the family Acanthaceae.

It is the only mangrove species naturally occurring in Victoria.

Where Melbourne’s mangroves grow

Grey mangrove occupies sheltered tidal mudflats around Port Phillip Bay and Western Port, where regular inundation, fine sediment and protection from strong waves create suitable habitat. VicFlora records it as locally common from the western half of Port Phillip Bay east to Corner Inlet.

The most accessible metropolitan example is Jawbone Marine Sanctuary beside Williamstown, about 20 minutes from central Melbourne. It protects the largest mangrove area within Port Phillip and Victoria’s only mangroves growing on a basalt coast. Other Port Phillip stands occur around Point Cook, Cheetham Wetlands and Avalon–Limeburners Lagoon.

The most extensive and well-developed Victorian communities occur in Western Port, including French Island, Yaringa, Churchill Island, Hastings, Lang Lang and Grantville. Victoria’s mangroves are among the world’s most southerly; the southernmost occurrence is recorded at Corner Inlet.

Victorian plants are usually compact—a shrub or small tree about 1–4 metres high—rather than the much taller trees found in warmer parts of the species’ range.

Built for salt, mud and tides

Living in waterlogged, salty, oxygen-poor mud demands a specialised toolkit:

Pneumatophores

Shallow lateral roots produce hundreds of upright, pencil-like roots. Air enters through pores in these pneumatophores when the tide is out, supplying oxygen to tissues buried in waterlogged, oxygen-poor mud.

Salt management

The roots limit salt uptake, and specialised glands on the leaves excrete excess salt. This allows the tree to live in saline water, although growth is often strongest in brackish conditions.

Sediment capture

The root network slows water movement, traps fine sediment and helps stabilise muddy shorelines. Sediment capture can also raise the wetland surface and change where mangrove and saltmarsh plants grow.

Flowers, fruit & dispersal

Small white-to-yellow flowers, produced during much of the year in Victoria, are visited by insects. Reproduction is described as cryptovivipary: the embryo begins developing while the fruit remains attached to the parent, but it stays enclosed by the fruit wall until dispersal. The buoyant fruit can float with the tide, shed its outer covering and establish when lodged on suitable wet mud.

This is more precise than simply calling the species viviparous. In “true” viviparous mangroves such as Rhizophora, the developing seedling visibly grows out through the fruit before it falls.

Why Melbourne’s mangroves matter

Mangrove stands are highly productive links between land and estuary. They:

  • provide shelter, feeding grounds and nursery habitat for fish and crustaceans;
  • support crabs, molluscs, insects and birds, including migratory shorebirds;
  • trap sediment and nutrients, reduce erosion and influence water quality; and
  • store substantial blue carbon, especially in their waterlogged soils.
Think in connected habitatsThese benefits depend on the condition of the entire coastal wetland—including adjacent mudflats, saltmarsh and seagrass—not on mangroves alone. Western Port’s internationally recognised Ramsar wetland is a particularly important example.

A restricted and changing habitat

Victoria’s mangroves are estimated to cover about 2,313 hectares. Despite being locally common at suitable sites, Avicennia marina subsp. australasica is listed as Endangered under Victoria’s Flora and Fauna Guarantee Act because its habitat is restricted and has been substantially modified.

Historical clearing and harvesting, coastal development, altered sediment movement, invasive species and erosion have affected mangrove communities. Climate change adds a different pressure: as sea level rises, mangroves and saltmarsh need room to migrate landward. Roads, seawalls, levees and other built structures can block that movement and squeeze coastal wetlands against the shoreline.

Mangroves can also expand into adjacent saltmarsh as water levels, sediment and climate change. That shift should not automatically be described as ecological recovery or degradation: both habitats have distinct values and need to be managed as a connected system.
